# Sections and rhetorical moves

Read the section that matches the task. The English examples use invented materials and do not supply evidence for a manuscript.

## Title and abstract

- Title: name the differentiating phenomenon, outcome, method or setting. Add the design or study population when it helps readers interpret the scope. Check the journal's format before deciding whether to use a claim as the title.
- Abstract: give a self-contained account of the problem and objective, design and material, decisive finding and bounded answer. Add quantitative magnitudes and uncertainty when available and relevant. For qualitative studies, name the central theme or interpretation and its evidentiary context. For a proposal, report the planned design and intended contribution in prospective language. Draft or check the abstract after the main argument is stable.
- Example objective: *We examined the association between opening hours and weekday use at three neighborhood libraries.*
- Example finding: *At the three sites, evening visits accounted for [verified fraction] of weekday use.*

Sources include [USC title](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/title), [USC abstract](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/abstract).

## Introduction and literature review

Use the CARS progression when it fits. Establish the subject and relevant knowledge, identify a defensible problem or gap, then show how the present work addresses it. State the specific question or hypothesis and preview the approach or main answer as the genre allows. Distinguish unresolved mechanisms, inconsistent evidence, untested settings and weaknesses of earlier measurements. Each supports a different rationale.

For a literature review, open each substantive paragraph with a synthesis claim. Group verified studies by evidence or conceptual relationship, explain disagreements through differences in design or context where supported, and connect the synthesis to the present question. Occasional study-by-study treatment is useful when a particular sequence or source is itself the point. Choose whether the author, date, study or finding should occupy the subject position based on the paragraph's emphasis. Use a named author when attribution or intellectual disagreement matters.

| Move | Original example |
|---|---|
| Establish relevant knowledge | *Prior surveys have described when residents use public libraries on weekdays.* |
| Specify the gap | *How evening opening hours relate to use by shift workers remains unclear.* |
| State the approach | *We address this question using attendance records paired with a patron survey.* |
| Synthesize agreement | *Across these surveys, access barriers cluster around travel time and opening hours.* |
| Explain a difference in coverage | *The regional survey counted occasional visits, whereas the site audit tracked weekly use.* |
| Position the contribution | *The paired records allow us to compare reported preferences with observed use at the same sites.* |

Sources include [USC introduction](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/introduction), [CARS](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/CARS), [Literature review](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/literaturereview), [Phrasebank introduction](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/introducing-work/), [Referring to sources](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/referring-to-sources/).

## Methods

Describe the design and why it answers the question. Spell out setting, data or recruitment, eligibility and sample, comparators, variables or coding, collection procedure, analytical choices, and relevant checks. Report missing-data handling, exclusion rules, ethics or consent, and software or materials when applicable and supported. Give information readers need to assess the inference or repeat the procedure. Separate planned analyses from exploratory deviations where the distinction matters.

- Rationale: *We selected sites with comparable catchment areas to reduce differences in baseline demand.* State how comparability was assessed when known.
- Eligibility: *The analysis included sites with complete hourly attendance records for the study period.*
- Definition: *We defined evening use as visits recorded after 18:00 local time.*
- Analysis: *We estimated the difference in mean visits per hour between the two opening schedules.*
- Qualitative procedure: *Two researchers coded the transcripts independently and resolved differences through discussion.* Use this only when it describes what happened.

Sources include [USC methodology](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/methodology), [Phrasebank methods](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/describing-methods/).

## Results

Match findings to aims or analyses. State the comparison and direction, report relevant magnitudes, units, denominators and uncertainty when available, then point to the figure or table that substantiates the statement. Highlight the pattern that answers the question. Report relevant null, adverse, unexpected and sensitivity findings. When reporting themes, distinguish participant statements, prevalence claims and the authors' interpretation, and identify the context for excerpts. In integrated Results and Discussion, place the observation before its explanation or implication.

- Quantitative result: *On weekdays, late-opening sites recorded [estimate] more visits per hour than early-closing sites (95% CI [limits]).*
- Imprecise result: *The estimate was near zero, with an interval spanning both decreases and increases of practical interest.* Check that the interval supports this wording.
- Figure callout: *The difference was concentrated in the final opening hour (Fig. 2).*
- Qualitative result: *Participants repeatedly described unreliable bus connections as a barrier to evening visits.* Check the coding evidence before stating frequency.

Sources include [USC results](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/results), [Non-textual elements](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/nontextual), [Phrasebank results](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/reporting-results/).

## Discussion

Start with the answer to the central question. For each important finding, interpret it, relate it to relevant verified evidence, and evaluate plausible explanations and alternative accounts. Describe what each material limitation changes about the interpretation, generalizability or recommendation. Close with the implications warranted by the study and a concrete next test when it follows from the evidence.

- Compare: *The concentration of visits in the final hour accords with the timing reported in [verified source].*
- Interpret cautiously: *The evening peak may reflect work schedules, a possibility the attendance records cannot resolve.*
- Scope: *Because the three sites share a transit network, the estimates may differ in areas with less frequent service.*
- Follow-up: *Linking visit times to patrons' work schedules could test whether shift patterns account for the evening peak.*

Sources include [USC discussion](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/discussion), [Limitations](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/limitations), [Phrasebank discussion](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/discussing-findings/).

## Conclusion

Return to the question and synthesize the answer, contribution, and conditions under which it applies. A concise closing usually needs only the relevant moves below. Use the end of Discussion when the venue combines the sections. Build the closing from results and analysis already presented.

| Move | Original example |
|---|---|
| Reconnect to the aim | *We assessed how evening opening hours relate to weekday visits.* |
| Synthesize the principal finding | *At the three sites, differences in use were concentrated late in the day.* |
| Draw a disciplinary implication | *This pattern is consistent with time constraints shaping access to library services.* |
| State the contribution | *Pairing records with patron surveys links observed visit times to reported access barriers.* |
| Specify a limitation | *The three sites share one transit network, which limits transfer to differently served areas.* |
| State a contribution within its limits | *Within this network, the records identify when additional opening hours were used.* |
| Propose a targeted next study | *Comparing sites on different transit networks could show whether service frequency modifies the pattern.* |
| Indicate practice or policy when supported | *These findings support piloting later closing times at similar sites and monitoring hourly use.* |

Sources include [USC conclusion](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/conclusion), [Phrasebank conclusion](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/writing-conclusions/).
