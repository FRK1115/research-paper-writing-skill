# Planning and evidence

## From a topic to a question

1. Identify the phenomenon, disagreement or practical problem and who needs an answer. Check what the assignment or journal asks the paper to do.
2. Run a preliminary scan to test whether the question is consequential, answerable and supported by accessible evidence. Read methods and results when judging a source's relevance to the question. Record definitions, methods, comparators, findings, constraints and unresolved disagreements.
3. Narrow a broad topic by changing one or two dimensions at a time: population, context, geography, period, outcome, relationships or comparison. Broaden an under-sourced topic with a neighboring concept, comparable setting or longer period. For a very recent event, use historical antecedents or comparable cases and mark current evidence as preliminary where appropriate.
4. Name the type of answer sought. A descriptive question asks about patterns or estimates. A comparative question asks how groups or conditions differ. A causal question needs a design and assumptions that address alternative explanations. An interpretive question asks how actors understand a process or how meaning arises in context. For completed studies, reconstruct the question that the actual design can answer and identify deviations from the original aims when relevant.

Working form: **Among [units] in [setting or period], what is [phenomenon, relationship or contrast] as measured or defined by [evidence]?** Omit irrelevant slots. Distinguish a topic from a research question, an answerable question from a testable hypothesis, and a hypothesis from a result.

## Design and inference

| Evidence or design | Ask before drafting the main claim |
|---|---|
| Cross-sectional survey or observational records | What population and time are represented? Which confounders, selection effects, measurement problems or temporal ambiguities matter? |
| Longitudinal data or cohort | What order or change can be observed? Could attrition, changes in measurement, or time-varying confounding affect the result? |
| Experiment or quasi-experiment | What comparison identifies the effect? How were units assigned or exposure groups formed? Which assumptions, fidelity issues and external-validity limits matter? |
| Exploratory, qualitative or case study | What sampling and interpretation support the themes or account? What counterexamples or sources qualify it? What are the conditions for transfer to other settings? |
| Historical or documentary study | How were records selected and evaluated? Which voices or events are missing? Does the source support the chronology, interpretation and attribution? |
| Review or synthesis | How were sources found and selected? What explains agreement or divergence? Match the claim of completeness to the search and review design. |
| Simulation, benchmark or method validation | What comparators, operating conditions and metrics were tested? Which assumptions or test sets limit transfer to practice? |

Use one row per substantive claim when planning or diagnosing a paper:

| Question or aim | Evidence and analysis | Finding or planned test | Warranted claim | Boundary or evidence gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What needs answering? | What source, outcome and comparison? | What is observed, or what will be evaluated? | What answer follows? | What assumption, scope or missing result limits it? |

Check that the question, unit of analysis and comparison match. Treat underlying validity issues as qualifications to the affected claim. If coding definitions, measurement windows, analytical assumptions or sampling are missing, request or mark the missing information before supplying specifics. Use effect sizes and uncertainty when available and preserve the author's statistical interpretation when justified.

## From outline to paragraphs

- Headings should reflect the work each section must do. For each paragraph, use a full-sentence claim or question as the outline item. Record the figure, result, passage or citation needed and the inference it supports.
- Organize multiple results by aims, comparisons or necessary analytical order. Organize a literature review by substantive themes, disputes, theories or methodological differences. Chronology earns its place when changes over time explain the argument.
- Reverse-outline a draft by writing one sentence per paragraph that describes what it actually accomplishes. Reorder paragraphs with unmet prerequisites, split paragraphs carrying unrelated claims and resolve unsupported transitions. Use transitions to make real relationships visible.
- For an empirical manuscript, orient the reader from the problem and gap to the actual study, then expand from findings to their meaning and boundary. For a thesis or course paper, adapt that progression to mandatory sections. For argumentative or conceptual work, develop the claim, evidence, counterargument and synthesis in an order suited to the argument.
